Type 02 · Proposed collection

Automation Failure Data in Rural Areas

Existing portals describe rural driving broadly. Our planned dataset isolates the safety-critical moments when automation degrades, disengages or requires driver takeover—and preserves the context before and after each event.

60

planned paired runs

3 fixed routes × 2 seasons × 5 repeated runs, collected in both human-driven and automated modes.

Estimated AV driving5–7.5 hours
  1. 01
    Choose failure-edge routes

    Prioritize unmarked, narrow, damaged, unpaved or flooded road segments.

  2. 02
    Capture rural long-tail events

    Animals, agricultural machinery, slow-moving vehicles and limited visibility.

  3. 03
    Build event-centered records

    Synchronize the system state, rural scene and driver response before, during and after each takeover.

  4. 04
    Repeat and compare

    Pair human and AV runs on the same routes across seasons and infrastructure changes.

Evaluation framework

What made the driver take over?

A takeover timestamp tells us when the driver intervened. The metrics below help explain why—whether the vehicle was running out of road space, approaching another vehicle, or losing system stability.

No single metric determines safety.

We use several signals together to find events that need review. The limits shown here are starting points from literature or simulation, not validated limits for our vehicle.

Question this metric answers

Is there enough time to keep the vehicle inside its lane?

TLC

TLC estimates how long it would take the vehicle to reach a lane boundary if its current motion continued without corrective steering.

How to read it

Smaller TLC = less time to react

If the driver reacts late, little time may remain to prevent a lane departure.

Candidate screening rule

TLCremaining < 0.5 s

Flag the event for closer review; calibrate with simulator and field data.

Show calculation and terms

Calculation

TLCremaining = TLCat failure − driver reaction time
